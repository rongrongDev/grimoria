# Multi-Region Reliability Architecture — Production Patterns & Common Pitfalls

**Applies to:** tool-agnostic (cloud-region examples are AWS-flavored, concepts port); extended-tier doc: patterns + pitfalls only · **Last verified:** 2026-07-14

**Scope note:** this doc covers the *reliability judgment* of multi-region — when it's worth it, what actually fails, how to know your failover is real. The build mechanics (DNS/anycast setup, replication configuration, IaC for region stamping) belong to `devops-engineer/` and your database's own documentation.

The opening question, which most orgs skip: **do you need this?** Multi-region roughly doubles infrastructure cost, adds a permanent tax on every architectural decision (every new feature must answer "what happens in a partition?"), and — the part nobody prices — introduces new failure modes that single-region systems don't have. I have seen more outages *caused by* half-committed multi-region machinery (split-brain, failover misfires, replication-lag surprises) than region outages prevented by it. The honest decision inputs: your SLO math (a 99.99%+ availability target cannot be met in one region because regions themselves don't deliver four nines — see `reliability-engineer/principles/slos-and-error-budgets.md` on dependency ceilings), your RTO/RPO for the region-loss disaster class (`reliability-engineer/topics/disaster-recovery.md`), data-residency law, and latency-to-user. If none of those forces it, a superb single-region system with cross-region *backups* beats a mediocre multi-region one on every axis, including — counterintuitively but consistently in my experience — availability.

## Production patterns

**Pattern: pick your topology by the data, not the compute.** Stateless compute is trivially multi-region; the entire problem is state. The three honest topologies:

| Topology | RTO/RPO character | The catch |
|---|---|---|
| **Active/passive (warm standby)** | RTO minutes-to-hours, RPO = replication lag | The passive region rots (see pitfalls); failover is a *decision* someone must make under uncertainty |
| **Active/active, partitioned data** (each user's data homed to one region) | Region loss affects only that region's users; per-user RTO = re-homing time | Cross-partition features (anything touching two users' data) become distributed-systems problems; re-homing under pressure needs to have been rehearsed |
| **Active/active, replicated data** | Best headline numbers | You have bought the CAP theorem retail: conflict resolution, write latency vs. consistency tradeoffs on every table, split-brain as a standing threat. Do not choose this to avoid *thinking*; it's the topology that requires the most |

**Pattern: failover is a product feature, tested like one.** The failover that works is: automated in mechanics, human-triggered in decision (full-region evacuation is a bet with a blast radius — a wrong-way failover during a false alarm is itself a SEV1; name who decides, per `reliability-engineer/topics/disaster-recovery.md` declaration pattern), and exercised quarterly *with production traffic* — evacuate a region on a calm Tuesday, serve from the survivors through peak, fail back, reconcile. If the org isn't willing to run that exercise, it doesn't believe in its own failover, and the multi-region spend is a costume. Netflix ran region evacuations monthly for years; that, not the architecture diagram, is what made theirs real.

**Pattern: capacity math includes the funeral.** Surviving region loss means the remaining regions absorb the dead one's load *plus* the failover thundering herd (every client reconnecting at once, caches cold). N regions at (N−1)-can-carry-peak utilization, with cold-cache surge headroom on top — this is the N+1 failover-surge rule from `reliability-engineer/principles/capacity-planning.md` at region scale, and it's the line item that makes active/active honest: two regions each running at 80% is a topology that *cannot* fail over, whatever the diagram says.

**Pattern: region-scoped observability and blast-radius-scoped alerting.** Every SLI sliced by region, always (`reliability-engineer/principles/observability.md` labeling standard); global aggregates hide a dead region behind three healthy ones until the fraction is huge. Alert on per-region SLO burn *and* on cross-region divergence (region A's error rate 10× region B's is a page-worthy asymmetry even when the global number looks fine). And monitor the replication lag as a first-class SLI with the RPO as its threshold — lag beyond RPO is the canonical irreversible-imminent cause-based alert (`reliability-engineer/principles/alerting.md`).

**Pattern: control plane out-of-region.** The failover tooling, DNS controls, deployment system, dashboards, and incident comms must not live (only) in the region being evacuated. Walk the evacuation runbook asking "does this step exist if us-east-1 is gone?" — same shared-fate audit as `reliability-engineer/topics/disaster-recovery.md`, and it fails the first time in every org I've watched run it.

## Common pitfalls

- **The rotting passive region.** The standby that hasn't taken traffic in a year has drifted: missing the config pushed manually to primary in March, the new dependency nobody replicated, capacity sized for last year's traffic. Detection: the quarterly evacuation exercise — nothing else detects it, because dashboards show the standby *up*, just not *sufficient*. Prevention: serve some real traffic from it continuously (even 5% keeps deployment pipelines, configs, and capacity honest), or accept that your RTO includes "fix the drift under fire."
- **Split-brain by ambiguous failover.** Region A looks dead *from region B* (a partition, not a death); B promotes itself; A is still serving its local users; two primaries accept conflicting writes, and the reconciliation is weeks of data surgery. Prevention: failover decisions from a third vantage point (external monitoring quorum, or a witness region), fencing on the demoted side (the old primary must *stop accepting writes* — a step every homegrown failover forgets), and a written reconciliation procedure drafted before you need it.
- **Global blast radius smuggled into a regional architecture.** The whole point is failure isolation, then someone adds a global config service, a single-region auth dependency, one DynamoDB global table in "just one" hot path, a deploy pipeline that pushes all regions in one wave — and a "regional" outage takes everything. Detection: for each shared component ask "if this misbehaves, how many regions feel it?"; anything answering "all" is a global dependency wearing a regional costume. Prevention: region-by-region deploy waves with bake time (mechanics in `devops-engineer/`, requirement owned here), and a standing architecture-review question for any new cross-region anything.
- **Testing failover, never failback.** The return trip is where reconciliation lives and where the second outage happens; an exercise that ends at "standby took traffic" tested half the runbook. (Same pitfall as DR restore-without-failback — `reliability-engineer/topics/disaster-recovery.md`.)
- **Latency physics denial.** Synchronous cross-region anything puts ~30–150 ms of speed-of-light in your write path, per round trip. Teams "add a region" and then wonder why p99 doubled: some call is quietly synchronous cross-region. Detection: trace-based (`reliability-engineer/principles/observability.md`) — spans crossing region boundaries in the request path. The fix is architectural (async replication, regional affinity), never a tuning flag.
