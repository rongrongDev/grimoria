# Disaster Recovery & Business Continuity — Production Patterns & Common Pitfalls

**Applies to:** tool-agnostic; extended-tier doc: patterns + pitfalls only · **Last verified:** 2026-07-14

**Working definitions** (full glossary: `reliability-engineer/GLOSSARY.md`): **RTO** — maximum tolerable time to restore service. **RPO** — maximum tolerable data loss, expressed as time ("≤ 5 minutes of writes"). A **disaster**, for this doc, is anything that takes out a whole failure domain your normal HA doesn't cover: region loss, data-corruption propagation, account compromise, the backup system itself failing.

The one-sentence truth of DR: **a recovery capability you haven't exercised recently does not exist.** It's a hope with a wiki page. Every DR plan I've seen fail — and I've seen four fail for real — failed on something no document review would catch: an IAM permission the restore role lost in a cleanup, a runbook step referencing a decommissioned bastion, a backup that had been silently empty for five months, a restore that worked perfectly and took nine days.

## Production patterns

**Pattern: RTO/RPO are negotiated business numbers, then validated against physics.** The business states what an hour of downtime and a minute of data loss actually cost, per system tier (not one number for everything — the payments ledger and the internal wiki do not share an RPO). Engineering then computes what the current architecture can actually deliver: your real RPO is your replication lag or backup cadence, whichever path survives the disaster class; your real RTO is *measured restore time*, not hoped. Where stated need and measured capability diverge, that gap goes to leadership as a costed choice — spend to close it, or sign the risk. Both are legitimate; the unexamined gap is not. Most orgs discover the gap during the disaster, which is the most expensive possible meeting.

**Pattern: restore-test on a calendar, with teeth.** Backups are write-only until proven otherwise. Quarterly minimum, per critical datastore: restore into a clean environment, run integrity checks, *time it*, record the result next to the RTO it's supposed to satisfy. Automate the routine version (continuous restore-verification is table stakes for tier-1 data); do the full human-executed exercise at least annually. A restore that succeeds but blows the RTO is a **failed test** — file it like a breach, because it is one, just pre-paid.

**Pattern: assume the people who wrote the plan are unavailable.** The disaster that takes out us-east-1 lands during the platform lead's honeymoon; the account-compromise scenario may *specifically* lock out your most privileged people. So: runbooks executable by an on-call generalist (game-day-test exactly that — hand the runbook to someone who's never run it and watch — see `reliability-engineer/topics/chaos-engineering.md`); break-glass credentials stored outside the systems they recover, tested on rotation; at least two people per critical recovery step who have *actually done it*, not read about it.

**Pattern: DR declaration is an incident-process branch, not a separate universe.** Who may declare "we're failing over" (name roles — failover under uncertainty is a bet with a cost, and someone must own it), what the criteria are, and what the communication cadence is — all of it plugs into the existing SEV1 machinery (`reliability-engineer/principles/incident-management.md`), because a disaster is a SEV1 with a bigger blast radius, and inventing a second process nobody rehearses guarantees the rehearsed one gets used anyway, badly.

**Pattern: alert on the DR posture itself.** Replication lag beyond RPO, backup job failures, backup *size anomalies* (the silent-empty-backup killer: the job "succeeds" writing 2% of yesterday's bytes), break-glass credential expiry. These are the canonical cause-based alerts that earn their place (irreversible + imminent + known action — `reliability-engineer/principles/alerting.md`): a failed backup is your RPO burning in real time with a defined fix.

## Common pitfalls

- **The untested runbook.** Fails in the first ten minutes on drift: renamed resources, revoked permissions, tooling versions, that one manual step in the author's head. Detection: the game day above — there is no other detection. Prevention: runbook exercises on the same quarterly calendar as restore tests; every exercise files the drift it found as immediate fixes, and a runbook that hasn't been exercised in 12 months is flagged un-trusted in its own header.
- **RPO/RPO theater — targets copied, not derived.** "RTO 4h / RPO 15m" pasted from a template into systems whose backup cadence is nightly (real RPO: 24h) or whose restore was last timed at never. Detection is one query and one awkward meeting: list stated targets vs. measured replication-lag/backup-cadence/restore-time per tier-1 system. Any row where stated < measured is theater. Fix: the negotiation pattern above.
- **DR that shares the failure domain it defends against.** Backups in the same region (or same cloud *account* — account compromise is a disaster class, and an attacker who owns the account owns same-account backups) as the primary; runbooks on the wiki that's down; the failover coordinator running in the region that failed; the on-call laptop's VPN requiring the SSO that's mid-outage. Walk the recovery path asking, at each step, "does this exist if the disaster is real?" Immutable/cross-account backup copies for tier-1 data; printed-or-offline copies of the top-3 runbooks; out-of-band comms channel agreed in advance.
- **Data corruption treated as an availability problem.** Replication is not backup: it replicates the corruption at line speed, and your standby is corrupt 200 ms after the primary. Corruption/ransomware scenarios need *point-in-time* recovery and — the part everyone forgets — a decision procedure for *choosing the restore point* (how do you find the last clean moment?) plus reconciliation for the writes after it. If your DR plan has one scenario and it's "region loss," this is the missing one, and it's the more common disaster.
- **Failover that's never failed back.** Teams test the failover and skip the return trip; the failback is where the data reconciliation lives (writes accepted during the split), and it's frequently the harder half. An exercise ends when you're back on primary with data reconciled, not when the standby takes traffic.
